What I Learned Rebuilding My Online Shortest-Path Research

March 31, 2026

A few years ago I worked on online shortest-path routing with machine-learned advice. The original thesis prototype was interesting, but like many early research codebases it mixed live API calls, notebooks, manual plotting, and a fragile evaluation setup. I recently rebuilt that work into a reproducible benchmark to understand what actually survives once the engineering becomes rigorous.

The Problem

The idea is simple: given several routing providers, each acting as an expert, which one should we trust for a given route query? Different experts may behave differently across cities, traffic conditions, weather, and time of day. The goal is to minimize the ratio between the travel time of the selected expert and the best expert available on that query.

What Changed in the New Repository

The new version replaces notebook-centric experimentation with a script-driven pipeline. It generates versioned artifacts, keeps train, validation, test, and OOD splits separate, and reports confidence intervals and failure cases. It also includes a short PDF report and tests, so the work is much easier to read and reproduce than the original prototype.

The key tradeoff is that the new benchmark is synthetic. The old thesis prototype used live provider responses from Rome and Paris, which made it closer to reality, but it was statistically weak and hard to reproduce. The new benchmark is the opposite: methodologically cleaner, but less realistic.

The Main Finding

The strongest result is not that a complex learned recommender solved routing. It is that the very simple min_eta heuristic remains extremely hard to beat.

Among the tested methods, the best non-oracle policy is a mixed strategy called RSv2, which switches between a contextual model and min_eta. It does come out on top, but only by a very small margin. That is the real finding: once the benchmark becomes more rigorous, extra model complexity buys only a tiny improvement.

Why This Matters

This is a useful negative result. Many ML systems look strong when compared to weak baselines or evaluated through noisy protocols. Here, the strong baseline is obvious, simple, and hard to displace. That is exactly the kind of result worth publishing honestly.

It also sharpens the real research question. If the benchmark only uses another expert as a proxy oracle, then we are not truly optimizing travel time in the real world. We are optimizing against the disagreement structure of a pool of providers. That is still useful, but it is a narrower problem than real traffic prediction.

What Comes Next

If I wanted to push this line of work further, I would not spend more time tuning synthetic models. The next meaningful step would be collecting real realized travel times and measuring how provider ETAs compare against actual trips. That would let us answer the questions that the synthetic benchmark cannot resolve:

  • When does min_eta fail in practice?
  • Which providers are miscalibrated, and under which conditions?
  • Does contextual expert selection still help once the labels are real?

Bottom Line

The old thesis was valuable because it showed that the problem had real-world motivation. The rebuilt repository is valuable because it makes the claims reproducible and harder to overstate. Put together, they tell a coherent story: expert selection for routing is interesting, but under the current setup the simple ETA-minimizing heuristic already captures most of the practical gain.

If you want to read the compact technical write-up, the repository now includes a short report and the full benchmark artifacts.
