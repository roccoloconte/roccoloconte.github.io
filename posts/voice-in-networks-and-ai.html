Voice in Modern Networks & AI

August 14, 2025

Understanding how voice travels through modern networks and AI systems reveals the sophisticated engineering behind seemingly simple conversations. From the moment you speak into a device to when someone else hears your voice, multiple layers of technology work together to preserve, compress, transmit, and recreate human speech with remarkable fidelity.

5G Networks

How Voice Gets Captured

When you speak into a 5G device, your voice undergoes a precise transformation process. The analog audio signal first passes through an analog-to-digital converter, which uses pulse-code modulation (PCM) to sample your voice at specific intervals — typically 8,000 to 48,000 times per second depending on the quality requirements.

Pulse-Code Modulation (PCM)

PCM works by taking snapshots of your voice wave at regular intervals, like photographing a moving object multiple times per second. Each snapshot measures the exact amplitude (loudness) of the sound wave at that precise moment. The converter then assigns a binary number to each amplitude measurement — for example, a 16-bit system can represent 65,536 different amplitude levels. This creates a stream of digital numbers that, when played back in sequence, recreates your original voice waveform. The sampling rate determines audio quality, the higher the better.

Modern 5G systems can sample at much higher rates than older networks, capturing more nuanced audio details including subtle emotional inflections and speaker characteristics that traditional telephony loses.

How Voice Gets Encoded

The digital signal then gets compressed using advanced codecs to reduce the amount of data that needs to travel across the network.

Audio Compression

Audio compression works by exploiting limitations in human hearing. The Enhanced Voice Services (EVS) codec, specifically designed for 5G, identify which audio frequencies humans can't perceive and removes them. For example, if a loud sound occurs at 1000 Hz, our ears become temporarily less sensitive to quieter sounds at nearby frequencies — the codec eliminates this masked audio data.

EVS dynamically analyzes your speech content in real-time, switching between different compression strategies. During silence or simple vowel sounds, it applies aggressive compression. When we speak complex consonants or multiple speakers talk simultaneously, it preserves more audio detail. This adaptive approach allows EVS to range from a few kbps for basic voice quality up to much higher kbps for near-studio quality audio.

Opus (not the Anthropic model), another key codec in 5G networks, excels at real-time applications like gaming or video calls. It can adapt its compression rate 50 times per second, responding almost instantly to changes in the voice or network conditions. This responsiveness explains why modern gaming voice chat feels so natural compared to older systems.

The encoded audio gets packaged into small data packets, each containing a few milliseconds of compressed speech plus routing information. The advanced processing capabilities of 5G devices and network hardware enable sophisticated compression algorithms that would have been too computationally intensive for older systems to handle efficiently.

How Voice Gets Secured

Before transmission, voice packets undergo encryption to protect against interception and eavesdropping. 5G networks implement multiple layers of security, including user plane encryption that scrambles voice data using algorithms like 256-bit AES. The Secure Real-time Transport Protocol (SRTP) adds another layer of protection specifically for voice communications, ensuring both confidentiality and integrity of the audio stream. This encryption happens transparently — users experience no delay or quality degradation, but their conversations remain protected from unauthorized access throughout the network journey.

How Voice Travels

Voice packets travel through 5G networks using Voice over New Radio (VoNR), a packet-based system that abandons the circuit-switched approach of older cellular networks. This represents the final evolution from circuit-switched networks in 2G and 3G systems, through Voice over LTE (VoLTE) in 4G networks, to the fully packet-switched VoNR approach in 5G. Instead of reserving a dedicated channel for your entire call, VoNR treats voice as high-priority data packets flowing through the same infrastructure handling internet traffic.

Quality of Service (QoS) mechanisms ensure voice packets get priority treatment. When network congestion occurs, your voice data jumps ahead of web browsing or file downloads. 5G networks can create dedicated network slices — virtual networks optimized specifically for voice applications with guaranteed latency and bandwidth parameters.

The packets don't necessarily follow the same path to their destination. Network intelligence routes each packet through the least congested route available, potentially sending consecutive voice packets through different cell towers. This dynamic routing helps maintain call quality even when parts of the network experience problems.

Edge Computing

5G's Multi-access Edge Computing (MEC) creates a critical middle ground between device and cloud processing. Voice processing can now happen at the network edge — closer to users than traditional cloud servers but with more computational power than individual devices. This enables AI-driven features like real-time translation, advanced noise suppression, or voice enhancement with latencies under 10 milliseconds, dramatically improving the user experience for voice applications without requiring powerful on-device hardware.

How Voice Gets Decoded

The receiving device collects these voice packets and reconstructs the original speech. This process requires sophisticated timing management since packets can arrive out of order or with varying delays. Jitter buffers temporarily store incoming packets, reordering them and smoothing out timing irregularities before playback.

When packets get lost or arrive too late, error concealment algorithms fill the gaps. Rather than creating obvious glitches, these systems predict what the missing audio should sound like based on surrounding speech patterns. Modern 5G implementations can recreate up to 20 milliseconds of missing speech so naturally that listeners rarely notice packet loss.

How Voice Gets Reproduced

The final step converts the reconstructed digital audio back to analog signals that drive speakers or headphones. Digital-to-analog converters (DACs) reverse the encoding process, reading the binary data and generating corresponding voltage levels that recreate the original waveform. Modern DACs in 5G devices use sophisticated interpolation algorithms to smooth the transition between digital samples, reducing distortion and artifacts.

High-quality DACs can reproduce frequencies from 20 Hz to 20 kHz — the full range of human hearing — compared to traditional telephony's 300-3400 Hz limitation. The analog output drives electromagnetic transducers in speakers or headphones, converting electrical signals back into the pressure waves we perceive as sound.

IoT Networks

IoT devices handle voice using fundamentally the same capture, encode, transmit, decode, and reproduce process as 5G networks, but with critical differences driven by power and computational constraints. IoT devices typically sample audio at lower frequencies with reduced bit resolution to conserve battery life.

They use codecs that are computationally lightweight to save power, such as G.711 (which is simple to process but uses 64 kbps bandwidth), or specialized low-bandwidth codecs when network constraints are the primary concern. Most IoT voice processing happens in the cloud rather than at the network edge, creating higher latency but allowing access to sophisticated AI models that would be impossible to run locally on battery-powered devices.

AI Voice Systems

AI voice systems use the same fundamental PCM capture and DAC reproduction processes as 5G networks — sampling analog audio into digital format and converting back to analog for playback.

Modern voice models have evolved beyond the traditional speech-to-text-to-speech pipeline. State-of-the-art conversational AI systems process audio input directly and generate audio output without text conversion, preserving prosodic information, speaker characteristics, and emotional nuance that text intermediates lose. This preservation allows AI systems to replicate human prosody — like laughter, sighs, or tones of sarcasm — creating audio that carries rich emotional cues lost in text-based synthesis.

Once captured through PCM sampling, modern AI systems convert digital audio to mel-spectrograms — visual representations showing how different frequencies change over time, essentially turning sound into a 2D image where the x-axis represents time and y-axis represents frequency. These mel-spectrograms provide a more effective input format for neural networks compared to processing raw audio waveforms directly.

Neural networks then process these mel-spectrograms through residual vector quantization (RVQ) or finite scalar quantization (FSQ), creating discrete tokens at rates like 40-75 tokens per second. This tokenization process is conceptually similar to how text gets broken into words or sub-words for language models, but here the "words" are small, information-rich chunks of sound that capture both acoustic details and semantic meaning.

The resulting learned encodings use transformer architectures with self-attention mechanisms, allowing models to focus on relevant acoustic segments while filtering noise. These systems process audio in overlapping time windows, typically 10-25ms segments, where each layer learns to detect increasingly complex patterns. Early layers identify basic acoustic features like frequency components and energy patterns, while deeper layers recognize phonemes, syllables, and eventually semantic meaning.

The decoding process actively generates new audio content following the same process in reverse: transformer models generate sequences of audio tokens, which get reconstructed into mel-spectrograms, from which digital audio is then synthesized and converted back to analog output.

On-Device AI Assistants

Current AI assistants like Siri still primarily use speech-to-text-to-speech architectures, but they face the challenge of running on local hardware with strict latency constraints (under 200ms). This requires model quantization — a technique that compresses neural networks while preserving functionality.

These optimized models sacrifice performance for real-time response, explaining why current on-device assistants haven't yet achieved the speech-to-speech capabilities of cloud-based systems. However, as mobile processors advance and machine learning research progresses, we're approaching the threshold where direct audio-to-audio processing will become feasible on consumer devices.
